GS Ballistics

Guide

User guide · English giorgio160586.github.io

GS Ballistics is an Android app for ballistic calculation in long-range shooting. It calculates the elevation needed to hit a target at a given distance, from the bullet's data, the weapon, the optic and the atmospheric conditions. The calculation is performed by numerically integrating the equations of motion, not with a fixed coefficient or an approximate formula. This guide describes the app's functions in Chapters 1-8 and the calculation principles of the ballistic engine in Chapter 9.

1Setting up the data for a calculation

Before a reliable elevation can be read, four values must be set, in the following order: the reticle's angular unit, the ammo in use, the atmospheric conditions and the target distance.

1.1Set the optic's reticle

The first value to define is the angular unit of the reticle mounted on the weapon: MOA or MRAD. It is set in the optics profile (Optics list → active profile), together with the click value (for example 0.25 MOA or 0.1 MRAD) and, if known, the turret's total travel. Every elevation the app calculates is expressed in this unit.

Fig. 1 — Optics list, with unit and click value

1.2Select the ammo

The elevation calculation depends on the ballistic coefficient (BC) of the bullet in use: without an ammo selected, or with an incorrect BC, the calculated value can't be trusted. Ammo is chosen from the bullet library or entered manually in the weapon profile, specifying caliber, weight, muzzle velocity and both the G1 and G7 ballistic coefficients. Where both are available, GS Ballistics defaults to the G7 model: the G7 table represents a long-ogive boat-tail projectile and is more accurate for the modern match bullets typically used in long-range shooting. The G1 model (flat-base projectile) remains preferable for bullets shaped closer to that reference, or when the manufacturer only publishes a G1 BC — in that case the drag model should be set to G1 in the weapon profile.

1.3Set air temperature and pressure

Air density and speed of sound, both used in the calculation, depend on temperature and atmospheric pressure: using values that don't match the real ones produces an elevation error that grows with distance. Temperature and pressure are set from the Settings screen and should be updated before every range session, based on the conditions measured in the field.

1.4Set the target distance

With the reticle, ammo and atmospheric conditions defined, the target distance is set from the dial on the Home screen. The elevation calculated for that distance appears immediately, in the unit defined in step 1.1. The distances offered on the dial are those of the active range: edit them from Range list → edit icon → "Distances" tab.

Fig. 2 — A range's "Distances" tab

1.5Set the shooting angle

Each target has its own uphill or downhill shooting angle, not the range as a whole: two targets on the same field can easily sit at different inclines, which is why the angle is set together with the distance, target by target, from the same "Distances" tab. An uphill or downhill shot needs less elevation than a level one at the same distance — it's the component of gravity along the line of sight, not the distance itself, that sets the drop — and the difference grows with both the angle and the distance.

When the Home dial is set to a distance that doesn't exactly match a saved target, the angle used in the calculation is interpolated between the two nearest saved targets; past the farthest saved target, it stays fixed at that one's angle instead of being extrapolated further.

  4. 4 Transonic reference thresholds (Mach 1.2 and Mach 0.8), computed from the speed of sound at the set temperature

At the standard reference temperature and pressure (15°C, 1013 hPa) the speed of sound is about 340 m/s (Mach 1) — it shifts slightly at a different temperature, which is why the chart recomputes the two dashed thresholds from the temperature set in Settings rather than from a fixed value. Based on that speed, every bullet's flight crosses three distinct bands:

  1. Supersonicabove ~410 m/s (past Mach 1.2)

    The bullet outruns the shockwave trailing behind it. This is the regime the G1/G7 drag tables are calibrated best for and behave most predictably in — the condition most precision shots are planned to still be in when they reach the target.

  2. Transonicroughly 410 to 270 m/s (Mach 1.2–0.8)

    The bullet slows to the point where the shockwave that used to run ahead of it catches up and passes through it. Drag coefficient shifts abruptly and less predictably, and gyroscopic stability — more than sufficient in the supersonic regime — can drop enough to produce noticeably wider dispersion or unpredictable deviation: this is the band where group size tends to open up in a less controllable way, regardless of how good the rest of the system is.

  3. Subsonicbelow ~270 m/s (under Mach 0.8)

    Past the transonic band, the bullet settles back into a relatively predictable regime again, with generally lower aerodynamic drag. This is the condition loads purpose-built to stay subsonic from the muzzle are designed around (typical of suppressed shooting); for a bullet that instead starts supersonic and only arrives here after slowing down along the flight, passing through the transonic band above has usually already eaten into its accuracy.

As for where to "sit" with your operating distance: the zone to avoid, when possible, isn't subsonic itself — it's band 2. The goal is to pick a maximum working distance where the bullet still arrives comfortably inside band 1 (solidly supersonic, with margin above the threshold), or, for loads purpose-built for band 3, to stay below its upper threshold the whole way. Crossing band 2 in flight isn't a problem if it happens well before the target; it becomes one when it happens right around the distance actually being shot.

8Exporting your data

The "Backup and restore" section in Settings (or the "Export report" entry in the main menu) exports a single HTML file containing every weapon profile, range and rifle setting. The file opens right away, in whichever HTML viewer is set on the device: from there, like any other page, sharing it to Drive, email, WhatsApp or saving it among the files is still one tap away.

The exported file is both a readable report and a complete backup at once. Opened in any browser it shows the active weapon profile with its ballistic table, drop chart and retained-velocity chart (with the same dashed transonic thresholds shown in chapter 6); every piece of data needed to restore the full configuration — including profiles not shown on screen — stays embedded in that same file, with no internet connection required. To restore it, on this device or another one, it's enough to import it from that same "Backup and restore" section.

9How the ballistic engine works

So far the app has been described from the outside, as whoever uses it sees it; it's worth a few more pages on what actually happens behind the number on screen, because understanding that also means understanding when to trust a calculated elevation and when it's worth verifying it on the range instead.

9.1Ballistic coefficient and drag models

Start with the number every shooter already knows best: the ballistic coefficient. In short, a bullet's BC is the ratio between its sectional density — mass divided by the square of the diameter — and its form factor relative to a standard reference projectile: the more "efficient" the real bullet is at cutting through the air compared to that reference, the higher the BC and the lower the drag at a given speed. GS Ballistics doesn't just apply that number as a fixed coefficient: it uses the standard G1 (flat-base projectile, the historically dominant convention) and G7 (long-ogive boat-tail projectile, more representative of modern long-range match bullets) drag tables, both indexed by instantaneous Mach number — the same historical Ingalls/McCoy curve the leading ballistic solvers are built on.

The BC a manufacturer publishes is a lot average, and can drift from that of your own specific lot or from the velocity your own barrel actually produces. Verifying it in the field just takes measuring the bullet's velocity at two points a known distance apart along the trajectory — the muzzle and 100 or 200 metres further out, say — with a chronograph, or with a single device able to record the whole trajectory in one shot. The velocity lost between the two points depends on the bullet's real BC: trying different BC values in the calculation until the predicted velocity at the second point matches the measured one gives you the actual BC of that exact rifle/load/lot combination — often more reliable than the factory-published one. Once found, it's just a matter of updating it in the weapon profile.

9.2Numerical integration, not a closed-form formula

Having the right table isn't enough if it then gets used with too simple a formula. Unlike analog slide-rule calculators or single-coefficient approximate formulas, the engine numerically integrates the equations of motion — air drag and gravity — step by step along the whole trajectory. At every step it recalculates velocity, Mach number and therefore the instantaneous drag coefficient from the G1/G7 table, instead of assuming from the start that the trajectory follows some already-known curve.

9.3Real atmosphere, not standard

Then there's a value that enters the calculation before the trajectory itself does: the air the bullet has to travel through. Air density and speed of sound — which sets the Mach number the drag table is indexed on — are derived in real time from temperature and pressure via the ideal gas law, instead of always assuming standard atmosphere (15°C, 1013.25 hPa). It's also, in practice, one of the places generalist ballistic software trips up most often: entering barometric pressure where absolute pressure is expected, or the other way around, produces an elevation error that stops being negligible well before long range.

9.4The geometry of a zero

One thing is still worth clearing up, and it's probably the most misunderstood: the elevation shown in the app isn't the bullet's raw drop, but the angular correction needed relative to the zeroed line of sight. It depends, then, on the scope's height above the bore and on the chosen zero range too, not on the trajectory alone — which is exactly why two rifles firing the very same load, but with scopes mounted at different heights, need different elevation at the same distance. These values — zero range, line-of-sight height, twist rate and any mount offset — are set from the "Scope and rifle configuration" screen, shared by every weapon profile.

If the scope height isn't already known, it can be measured with a caliper, no disassembly required: measure the outer diameter of the scope's bell where it sits in the ring, and the barrel's diameter at the same point, halve each of the two, then add the free gap between the underside of the bell and the top of the barrel, also read off the caliper. The sum of the three values is the height being measured.
